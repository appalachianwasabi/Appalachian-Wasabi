Wasabi Facts/FAQ

What real wasabi is, and why it’s so rare.

Nineteen facts and six common questions, each checked against published sources. Bracketed numbers link to the source list at the bottom of the page.

Nineteen facts

  1. It’s a rhizome, and the whole plant is edible.

    What gets grated is the rhizome, an underground stem. The leaves and flowers are edible too. [1]

  2. Real wasabi is Eutrema japonicum.

    It was long classed as Wasabia japonica. It belongs to the Brassicaceae family, alongside horseradish, mustard, cabbage, broccoli and kale. [1][2]

  3. It’s Japanese in origin and well over a thousand years old.

    The plant is native to Japan, the Russian Far East (including Sakhalin) and the Korean Peninsula. The oldest record of wasabi as food dates to the 8th century, and one source traces its use in Japan to 794 CE. [1][2]

  4. One rhizome takes roughly two years to grow.

    Sources give a range of about 18 months to 3 years. Utah State Extension puts full size at around two years, for a rhizome of 4–6 inches. One Canadian greenhouse grower reports cutting the time to 15 months with hydroponics. [1][3][6][7]

  5. It wants cool, dark and wet.

    Utah State Extension recommends a growing season of 50–60°F, water at 45–55°F, and at least 75% shade during the day. Other sources give similar numbers in Celsius (air 8–18°C, water 10–13°C). [2][3]

  6. Clean, moving water is a big part of the recipe.

    Wasabi is semi-aquatic. It needs cool water bathing the plants year-round but does badly in standing water. In Japan, the traditional stream-fed method is called sawa wasabi. [3][4][7]

  7. It’s hard to start from seed.

    Growers usually propagate from side shoots that form on the rhizome, which produces genetically identical plants. [1][3][6]

  8. Pests and disease are constant concerns.

    Aphids, slugs and snails are the common pests, and the plant is prone to fungal and bacterial stem and root diseases. In early North Carolina trials, varieties suffered from Phytophthora root rot and Rhizoctonia crown rot, and plants in heavy shade fared better. [3][5]

  9. Appalachia suits it, with careful management.

    NC State Extension notes that parts of western North Carolina have a climate remarkably similar to Japan’s, and another report says the region meets wasabi’s temperature needs about 75% of the year. Western North Carolina is among the places where wasabi is grown commercially in the U.S. [2][5][6]

  10. Most “wasabi” outside Japan isn’t wasabi.

    The green paste served with most sushi is typically horseradish and mustard, colored with food dye or spinach powder. One industry report estimates a standard packet holds about 0.5% real wasabi at best. [1][2][4][7]

  11. Real and imitation differ in taste and texture.

    Fresh wasabi is gritty, fresher, sweeter and more fragrant, with a natural green that varies by cultivar. Imitation tends to be a smooth, thick paste that is harsher and lingers longer. [1][8]

  12. The heat hits your nose, not your tongue.

    The pungency comes from isothiocyanates, mainly allyl isothiocyanate, which forms when the plant’s cells are broken during grating. It stimulates the nose more than the tongue and activates a receptor called TRPA1, nicknamed the “wasabi receptor.” [1][2][4]

  13. The burn fades in minutes.

    Freshly grated wasabi begins losing its punch after roughly 10–15 minutes, which is why it’s grated just before serving. Horseradish, by contrast, stays hot for hours. [1][4][8]

  14. Wasabi and horseradish are cousins with different chemistry.

    Both contain allyl isothiocyanate. Horseradish also contains 2-phenylethyl isothiocyanate, which wasabi lacks entirely. [4]

  15. Traditionally, it’s grated on sharkskin.

    The classic tool is a piece of dried shark skin on a wooden paddle. Modern kitchens often use fine metal graters called oroshigane, and premium restaurants grate it to order. [1][2][8]

  16. A wasabi smoke alarm won an Ig Nobel Prize.

    The 2011 Ig Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Makoto Imai and colleagues for a fire alarm that releases wasabi vapor to wake people who can’t hear a conventional alarm. [2][10]

  17. It’s priced like a specialty crop.

    A 2018 industry report cited about US$220 per kilogram for Canadian-grown wasabi and £250 per kilogram in the UK. Another source says high-end restaurants pay up to $300 per kilogram, against under $5 for horseradish. Prices vary widely by market and year. [7][8]

  18. Japan’s main growing regions are in the mountains.

    The leading prefectures are Nagano, Iwate and Shizuoka (including the Izu Peninsula). Japan’s total output in 2016 was about 2,265 metric tonnes. Wasabi is also grown commercially in Taiwan, South Korea, New Zealand, Canada and the U.S. [2]

  19. It’s about flavor, not nutrition.

    Raw wasabi root is about 69% water, 23.5% carbohydrate and 4.8% protein. Wasabi paste itself isn’t especially nutrient-dense. Restaurant versions, which are usually imitation, can be high in sodium for their serving size. [2][9]

Common questions

Is real wasabi hotter than horseradish?

No. Real wasabi is generally described as milder, sweeter and more complex than horseradish. The sensation arrives quickly and fades within minutes, while horseradish can linger for hours. [4][8]

How can I tell if it’s the real thing?

Read the ingredients. If you see horseradish, mustard or green coloring, it isn’t pure wasabi. Real wasabi is freshly grated, slightly gritty, and never prepared in advance. [1][8]

How should I use fresh wasabi?

Grate it right before serving and use it within minutes, since the flavor fades quickly. The leaves, rhizome and flowers are all edible. [1]

Why is real wasabi so expensive?

It takes around two years to produce a harvestable rhizome and needs clear, cool running water. Growers also contend with disease, late freezes and flooding, and the plant is brittle. One North Carolina grower has described it as no way to get rich quickly. [3][4][7]

Can wasabi really be grown in Appalachia?

Yes. Extension researchers in western North Carolina found that wasabi will grow there, but they recommend disease-resistant varieties, shade, and controlled water systems rather than relying on natural streams that can flood or run dry. [5][6]

I have a soy allergy. Should I be careful with wasabi?

Check the label. Imitation wasabi products often contain soybean oil, so if you have a soy allergy, avoid them unless you have confirmed what’s in them. For medical questions, ask your doctor. [9]
